      Part 1

      Inferential statistics

      How a sample lets you say something about a whole population, and how sure you can be.

      L5 · 01

      From a sample to a population

      Descriptive statistics summarize the data you have. Inferential statistics use that data to say something about a bigger group you never fully measure. A number describing the whole population is a parameter, like the population mean μ ("mu"), which is usually unknown. A number computed from your sample is a statistic, like the sample mean x̄ ("x-bar"). n is the sample size.

      Who is in your sample?

      In this simulation the classroom only contains commuters (the long right cluster). Repeat the random sample a few times: it wobbles around μ. The classroom sample is consistently too high, and making it bigger does not fix that.

      Remember

      • Descriptive: "here is what happened." Inferential: "here is what we can infer."
      • Every method in Lecture 5 assumes a random sample from the population you care about.
      • A bigger sample (larger n) from a biased source just gets you a more confident answer that is still wrong.

      Common mistake

      The class's average commute (42 min) describes students like the ones in that class (one upper-level IS section), not necessarily all NJIT students. No calculation later in the lecture can fix a sample that was not random.
      L5 · 02

      Probability as area, and the normal curve

      For values like times and scores, you ask about ranges. The probability of a range is the area under the density curve over that range, and the total area is 1. A normal curve is set by two numbers: the mean μ (center) and the standard deviation σ (spread). The z-score z = (x − μ) / σ says how many σ a value sits from the mean.

      Remember

      • 68–95–99.7: share of values within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations (SD, σ) of the mean. The exact 95% cutoff is 1.96σ. Remember 95% and 1.96: they come back in concepts 05 and 10.
      • The curve's height is not a probability. Only area is.
      • |z| > 3 happens only 0.3% of the time, which is why Lecture 3 used it to flag outliers.
      • Most real variables are not normal: taxi fares, commutes and incomes are right-skewed. Concept 03 explains why normal-based tests still work.

      Common mistake

      "Above 90" is one tail only. 5% falls outside ±2σ in total, so only about 2.5% is above 90 (exactly 2.28%).
      L5 · 03

      The Central Limit Theorem

      Take a sample of size n, compute its mean, and repeat many times. Even when the population is skewed, those sample means pile up into a roughly normal shape centered on μ. The pile gets narrower as n grows.

      Sampling from a skewed population

      μ = 3, σ = 3

      Samples drawn–
      Mean of the x̄'s–
      SD of the x̄'s–
      σ / √n predicts–

      Left: the population, with the latest sample's values marked. Right: every sample mean so far. The dashed curve is the normal shape the CLT predicts. Try n = 1 (no averaging, so it looks like the population) and then n = 30.

      Remember

      • The CLT gives the shape of the sampling distribution: normal.
      • It is centered on the population mean μ, not the median.
      • Its spread is σ/√n, the standard error (next concept).
      • With about 30+ values per sample, moderate skew is fine. Small and skewed data needs different tests (concept 08).

      Common mistake

      The CLT is about the distribution of sample means. The raw data stay just as skewed as before.
      L5 · 04

      Standard error

      The standard error measures how much a statistic would bounce around if you repeated the study with the same sample size. For a mean, SE = s / √n, where s is the standard deviation of your sample and n is the sample size. The square root makes precision expensive: to halve the SE you need four times the data.

      SE shrinks as n grows (s = 10)

      SE–
      Last change–

      Remember

      • SD describes how spread out individual values are. SE describes how spread out the sample mean is.
      • Taxi fares: SE is $1.16 with 100 rides, so $0.58 with 400 rides.
      • The Central Limit Theorem (CLT) gives the shape and the SE gives the width. Together they power every test in this course.

      Common mistake

      Doubling n does not halve the SE. It divides it by √2, about 1.41.
      L5 · 05

      The 5-step hypothesis test

      A hypothesis test asks one question: if nothing were going on, how surprising would my data be? You assume "no effect" (H₀), measure how far your data land from it, and reject H₀ only if that result would be rare.

      The steps, in order

      1. 1
        Formulate hypotheses

        H₀ says there is no difference and always uses "=" (μA = μB). H₁ says there is one: "≠" for a two-tailed test. This course always uses two-tailed tests.

      2. 2
        Choose α

        The significance level, usually 0.05. Confidence level = 1 − α = 95%. Choose it before looking at the data.

      3. 3
        Choose the test and compute the statistic

        The statistic (t, z, χ²) measures how far the sample is from what H₀ predicts.

      4. 4
        Find the p-value

        The probability of a result this extreme or more extreme, assuming H₀ is true.

      5. 5
        Decide

        p ≤ α: reject H₀. Otherwise: fail to reject H₀. You never "accept" H₀.

      Is this coin fair? 100 flips

      H₀: P(heads) = 0.5
      p-value (two-tailed)–
      Decision at α = 0.05–

      The bars show how often each count happens with a truly fair coin. Red bars are results at least as far from 50 as yours, in either direction. Their total height is the p-value. The slide's example of 85 heads is astronomically unlikely, so you reject "fair".

      Remember

      • H₀ is the "no effect" assumption, so it always gets the equals sign. Never write H₀: μA ≠ μB.
      • Reject H₀ → the groups differ, and the means tell you which direction.
      • Fail to reject → no statistically significant evidence of a difference. That is not proof there is none; the sample may be too small.

      Common mistake

      With α = 0.05 and p = 0.12 you fail to reject H₀. You may not conclude the two designs are the same.
      L5 · 06

      The t-statistic and the p-value

      A t-statistic is signal ÷ noise: the difference in means divided by its standard error. A bigger difference or less noisy data gives a bigger t. You then check where t lands on the t-distribution for your degrees of freedom (df = n − 1 for one sample or paired data). Small samples have heavier tails, so they need a bigger t to reject.

      Remember

      • p-value = area under the t-curve beyond ±|t|. α = the total red area you agreed to call "too rare".
      • t lands in the red tails ⟺ p < α ⟺ reject H₀.
      • As n → ∞ the t-distribution becomes the standard normal, and t* approaches 1.96.
      • Statistical significance ≠ effect size. A huge sample can make a tiny, useless difference "significant". Report both.

      Common mistake

      A p-value is not the probability that H₀ is true. It is computed by assuming H₀ is true.
      L5 · 07

      Type I and Type II errors

      Every decision can be wrong in two ways. A Type I error rejects a true H₀ (a false positive), and you set its rate up front with α. A Type II error misses a real effect (a false negative). Its rate, β, depends on how big the real effect is and how much data you have.

      Remember

      • Larger α → more false positives, fewer false negatives.
      • Smaller α → fewer false positives, more false negatives.
      • More data (or a bigger real effect) pulls the two curves apart, which raises power without touching α.

      Common mistake

      "Not significant" often just means low power. An underpowered study can easily miss a real effect.
      L5 · 08

      Which test do I use?

      Three questions pick the test: what type of data, how many groups, and are the same people measured twice. This course works with the three t-tests for comparing means; the others are on the map so you can recognize them.

      Test picker

      1 · What are you measuring?
      2 · How many groups?
      3 · Same people measured in both conditions?
      USE–

      Practice: name the test, then open to check

      Remember

      • One-sample t: one group vs. a known value.
      • Paired t: the same people in two conditions. It works on each person's difference, which should be roughly normal.
      • Independent t: two different groups that are otherwise alike. Use the unequal-variances (Welch) version by default.

      Common mistake

      Before/after on the same customers is paired. Before/after where the "before" is only a known average is one-sample.

        L5 · 10

        Confidence intervals

        A confidence interval is a range of plausible values for a parameter: x̄ ± t* × SE. A 95% CI holds exactly the null values that a two-tailed test at α = 0.05 would not reject. That makes it a test and an effect-size estimate in one picture.

        What "95%" actually means

        20 samples of n = 10 from a population with μ = 50
        In this batch–
        All intervals so far–

        Each line is one study's 95% interval. Red ones missed the true μ. Over many studies about 95% catch it. Any single interval either contains μ or it doesn't.

        CI ↔ hypothesis test

        H₀ says the mean difference is 0. If 0 is outside the 95% CI, reject at α = 0.05. If 0 is inside, fail to reject.

        Back to our commutes

          Remember

          • 95% describes the procedure: across repeated samples, about 95 of 100 intervals contain μ.
          • Two group CIs that don't overlap → significant. If they overlap, the result is ambiguous: check the CI of the difference.
          • For paired data (like concept 09), use the CI of the differences, not two separate group CIs.
          • CIs show the plausible size of the effect, which is often more useful than p alone.

          Common mistake

          "There's a 95% probability μ is in this interval" is wrong. The 95% is the long-run hit rate of the method.

          Pitfalls and misconceptions

          A p-value is only honest if you chose the comparison before looking at the data. Every extra split (by gender, by age, by year) is another test, and another chance for pure noise to come out "significant".

          Real-world pitfalls

          • p-hacking: running tests until one is significant by chance.
          • Small-sample bravado: sweeping conclusions from n = 12.
          • Ignoring effect size: p = 0.001 for a 0.5% improvement may not be worth anything.
          • Correlation ≠ causation: tests measure association. Causal claims need experiments.

          For your project

          Decide the comparison before testing. Say how many tests you ran. Report effect size and CI, not only p. Check whether a pooled result holds within subgroups (concept 16).

          Visualization

          Why we plot data, how perception reads a chart, and how to choose one that answers the question honestly.

          L4 · 12

          Why visualize: Anscombe's quartet

          These four datasets have the same means, the same variances and the same correlation. Plotted, they are completely different: a noisy line, a curve, a perfect line with one outlier, and a vertical stack held up by a single point. Summary statistics can be too reductive.

          Why visualize

          • Statistics can hide shape. Look at the data, not only the numbers.
          • You can explore data before you know which question to ask.
          • A chart acts as external memory: you compare by looking instead of holding numbers in your head.
          • Three uses: exploration (generate hypotheses), confirmation (check them), communication (present them).

          The bar to clear

          At the very least, a visualization should be more effective than a table.
          L4 · 13

          Marks and channels

          Marks are the shapes that stand for data: points, lines, areas and bars. Channels are the visual properties that carry the values: position, length, angle, area, color, shape. Your eyes read some channels far more accurately than others, so the channel you pick decides how well people can compare.

          Same five market shares, three channels

          Without the values: is B bigger than A? Which company is third? Switch channels and notice when the answer becomes obvious.

          Remember

          • Bar chart: mark = bar, channel = length. Scatter plot: mark = point, channels = horizontal and vertical position.
          • Match the channel to the data type: magnitude channels for ordered values, identity channels for categories.
          • Use 3–4 channels at most before a chart gets cluttered.

          Common mistake

          Pie charts use angle, which is hard to compare. Close slices, and especially several pies side by side, are where they fail. Bars on a shared axis are almost always better.
          L4 · 14

          Start from the question

          Don't start with a chart type. Start with the question you're asking, then pick the mark, the channel, and finally the chart.

          What is your question?

          Exploratory vs explanatory

          • Exploratory: you are still looking for the story. Many views, filters, detail.
          • Explanatory: you are presenting a finding. Few variables, one message per chart, focus on what answers the question.
          • Presentations are usually explanatory. Dashboards default to explanatory but add filters to drill down.

          Where the classic chart chooser is outdated

          For share of total, use sorted or stacked bars, not a pie. For three variables, use color or size on a scatter plot, or small multiples, not 3D.
          L4 · 15

          Honest charts: the right mark and a true zero

          Two common mistakes distort what a reader sees. A line drawn across categories implies a connection or order that isn't there. A bar axis that doesn't start at zero breaks the length encoding, so differences look many times bigger than they are.

          Remember

          • Lines are for ordered data, usually time. Bars compare amounts across categories.
          • Horizontal bars make long labels readable. Sorting makes the ranking obvious.
          • Bars need a true zero. For a non-zero baseline, use dots or lines instead.
          • Watch real-world charts for uneven axis spacing, dates out of order, and pie "slices" that sum past 100%.

          Common mistake

          Starting the mpg axis at 10 makes the Corolla's bar about 60× the Cadillac's, when its mileage is only 3.3×.
          L4 · 16

          Simpson's paradox: the overview can mislead

          A comparison that holds overall can reverse once you split by a lurking variable. Start with an overview, but don't end with one: dig into the variables that could explain it.

          Kidney stone treatments, 350 patients each

          Charig et al., BMJ 1986
          Open surgeryPercutaneous (keyhole)

          Remember

          • Open surgery wins for small stones and for large stones, yet loses overall.
          • The reason: open surgery mostly got the harder large-stone cases (263 of its 350 patients).
          • A significant pooled difference can reverse within subgroups. Check before you conclude.

          Common mistake

          Reading the pooled bars alone, you would pick keyhole surgery, which is worse for every type of patient.

          Rules of thumb, applied

          The same data can be a wall of numbers or a clear argument. The redesign below removes clutter, uses gray for context and one color for the point, picks a line because this is a trend over time, and annotates the event that explains it.

          IT ticket volume, one year

          • 1 · Maximize the data-ink ratioRemove borders, shadows and backgrounds; lighten gridlines. Annotations and highlights are not clutter.
          • 2 · Use color sparingly and meaningfullyStart in grayscale, add color only to highlight or to encode categories. 6–7 colors max, no rainbow scales.
          • 3 · Preserve perceptual accuracyChoose effective channels. Never truncate a bar axis. Be wary of relative changes.
          • 4 · Provide contextReference lines (averages, targets, prior periods), consistent scales, annotations for key events.
          • 5 · Organize for comparisonGroup related items, keep ordering consistent, align shared axes, prefer small multiples to one complex chart.
          • AlsoKnow your audience and task. No 3D unless it conveys real shape. Overview first, zoom and filter, details on demand (Shneiderman). Tell a story, honestly.

          Three kinds of color scale

          CategoricalNominal data. Distinct hues, no order implied. Six or seven at most.
          SequentialOrdered or quantitative. One hue, light = low, dark = high.
          DivergingQuantitative with a meaningful midpoint: zero, the mean, a target.

          About 1 in 12 men of European ancestry has red-green color vision deficiency. Never let red versus green alone carry the meaning.
